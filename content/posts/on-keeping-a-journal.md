A journal is not a record of events. It is a practice of thinking — a place where the half-formed becomes clear.

## Writing to Understand

Most of us do not know what we think until we write it down. The act of putting words in sequence forces a kind of honesty. Vague anxieties become specific problems. Specific problems become questions. Questions, sometimes, become answers.

This is not therapy, though it may feel like it. It is closer to philosophy — the examined life made slightly more legible.

## What to Write

There are no rules. But some prompts that have proven useful:

- What occupied my mind today, and why?
- What am I avoiding?
- What would I advise a friend in my situation?
- What was beautiful today, even briefly?

You do not need to answer all of these. One is often enough.

## On Rereading

The strangest gift of a journal is the past self it preserves. To read an entry from three years ago is to meet someone you recognise but can no longer entirely inhabit. Their worries feel distant. Their certainties, touching in their naïveté.

This, too, is useful. The troubles that consume us rarely consume us forever. The journal is evidence of that — proof that we have survived every difficult day so far.

## A Note on Medium

Paper or screen, it matters less than consistency. What matters is a place reserved for thinking, returned to regularly, without the pressure of an audience.

Write badly. Write honestly. Write when you do not feel like writing. That is when it matters most.
